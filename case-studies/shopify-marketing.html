Building Shopify's Merchant Marketing Platform

Shopify (2017–2023) · Staff Product Designer

Over six years on Shopify's Merchant Marketing team, I took the Marketing Tab from a handful of ad integrations to a full merchant growth platform. Then I rebuilt its analytics from the ground up once merchants could finally run sophisticated marketing but had no way to tell if any of it was working. The platform work alone drove a 52% increase in sales attributed to marketing activity.

Problem

Key Finding

In Shopify's Annual Merchant Survey, 91% of merchants said their biggest challenge was marketing their business.

When I joined Shopify in 2017, the Marketing Tab was just a handful of ad integrations: Google, Facebook, and little else. Merchants had strong commerce fundamentals (inventory, payments, fulfilment) but nothing built to help them grow beyond those two channels. If a merchant wanted to send an email or set up an abandoned cart flow, they were doing it across unconnected third-party tools, with no way to see the combined effect.

Marketing is one of the most intimidating activities for a small business owner: it requires confidence in channels you don't fully understand, budgets you're nervous to spend, and results that are hard to interpret. Merchants didn't know where to start, what was working, or how their scattered activities connected to each other.

Shopify's opportunity was clear: become the place where merchants ran their store and grew it. My job was to figure out what that looked like, and then keep figuring it out as the problem evolved over six years.

What we uncovered

Research Foundation

17-participant, six-month diary study, long enough to watch how merchants' marketing needs actually shifted across a full season.

Merchants don't think in "channels"; they think in goals

Reframed design around merchant goals instead of the platform they were using

Marketing is a loop, not a one-time action

Shaped the Marketing Mental Model (Trigger → Goal → Build → Act → Measure → Learn), a framework shared across PM, design, and research

Phase 1

Ad Integrations

The Marketing Tab launched with ad integrations, so merchants could run ads without leaving the Shopify admin. The hard part was the setup behind Google and Facebook: business accounts, domain verification, two-factor auth, pixel tracking, catalog feeds and ad category restrictions.

The design challenge was making very different systems, each with its own data model and terminology, feel like one Shopify-native surface.

admin.shopify.com/marketing/integrations

Phase 2

Shopify Email

The next step was to build email natively instead of partnering with Mailchimp or Klaviyo. Email converts the most customers and holds the richest segment data, so owning it let us connect it closely to commerce data. That set up the automations we shipped later.

I did the initial discovery, then handed Shopify Email to a new team. Our two teams kept shaping the Marketing Tab, especially email previews and a visual editor in the integration flow.

admin.shopify.com/marketing/email/new

The Next Challenge

Every tool we added over the years gave merchants more ways to grow, but it also introduced a new problem. Merchants were running ads, email, and promotions as separate, self-contained units. Our research showed that the best merchants planned in multi-step plays anchored to a single goal, not individual channels. Campaigns became the thread that connected everything a merchant was doing toward that goal.

Phase 3

Campaigns

The first version of Campaigns let merchants group related activities, like an ad, an email and a discount, under one name and goal. A merchant running a Father's Day promotion could see everything tied to that moment in one place: audience ads, retargeting, email sends and page posts, with dates, spend and status.

The challenge was making several channels, each with its own data and lifecycle, feel coherent on one screen.

admin.shopify.com/marketing/campaigns

Phase 4

Templates

Research showed our best merchants ran the same playbooks again and again, such as a mix of ads, email and post-purchase flows for each big sale. Templates turned those playbooks into a feature every merchant could use.

A merchant planning Back-to-School could start from a template with the goal, channel mix, timeline and tactics already set, then adjust it. The question moved from "how do you create a campaign?" to "how do you make this strategy your own?"

admin.shopify.com/marketing/templates

Phase 5

Automations

Many merchants' marketing already ran on autopilot: triggered actions, like abandoned cart or win-back emails, that fire based on what a customer does. Automations brought that logic into the Marketing Tab so merchants could set it up once.

The challenge was range. Automations run from one step to complex branching flows, so we built two levels: pre-built automations to start fast, and a workflow builder for full control.

admin.shopify.com/marketing/automations

The Next Challenge

By this point, the Marketing Tab could run everything: ads, email, automations, templates. But its analytics were still rudimentary. They couldn't answer a simple question: how a campaign performed as a whole, across every channel, including the ones running outside Shopify.

Phase 6

Campaign Reports

Before this, merchants saw each ad or email report on its own, or one report per channel, with no view of the whole campaign. Campaign Reports put it in one place: goals against actuals, a channel breakdown, performance over time and attribution context, so merchants could see what drove results and decide where to spend next.

admin.shopify.com/marketing/reports/bfcm-2022

Phase 7

Unified Marketing View

Unified Marketing brings every campaign into one table, whether it ran fully in Shopify, partly in Shopify, or came in from an outside platform through UTM parameters. Status, KPIs, channel mix and spend are visible at a glance.

Attribution was the hardest part. Each channel counted clicks and sales its own way, and the numbers disagreed. We chose one shared model for Shopify's view and showed that model and its data source beside the numbers, so merchants could trust the data even when it didn't match what they saw in each channel.

admin.shopify.com/marketing
